          Cybersecurity Fundamentals

          What is Cybersecurity?

          Protecting devices, accounts, networks, and data from digital attacks.

          Definition: Cybersecurity (sometimes written "cyber security") is the practice of protecting computers, phones, servers, networks, and the data on them from unauthorized access, damage, theft, or disruption. It's a broad field that includes everything from the password on your phone to the firewalls protecting a hospital's patient records.

          Why it matters: Almost every part of daily life now runs through connected devices and accounts — banking, healthcare, communication, work, and shopping. Cybersecurity exists because that convenience creates opportunity for criminals: a single stolen password can lead to drained bank accounts, stolen identities, or a business shutting down entirely.

          The three main goals of cybersecurity are usually summarized as protecting confidentiality (keeping information private), integrity (keeping information accurate and untampered), and availability (making sure systems and data are accessible when needed) — this is known as the CIA Triad, covered in its own lesson below.
          Example: Using a strong, unique password and two-factor authentication on your email is a basic but powerful cybersecurity practice — it stops attackers from reading your messages or using your email to reset the passwords on your other accounts (a common attack technique called an "account takeover chain").
          Remember: Cybersecurity isn't just for tech experts, IT departments, or big companies — everyone who uses a phone, email, or the internet needs basic cybersecurity habits. Most successful attacks target ordinary individuals, not corporations.

          Types of Cyber Threats

          Malware, phishing, ransomware, social engineering, and more.

          Main threat categories, explained:

          Phishing — fake emails, texts, or messages designed to trick you into giving up passwords, card numbers, or clicking a malicious link.
          Malware — an umbrella term for any harmful software, including viruses, spyware, and ransomware.
          Ransomware — malware that locks or encrypts your files and demands payment (usually cryptocurrency) to get them back.
          Social engineering — psychological manipulation that convinces people to break normal security procedures, often by impersonating someone trustworthy.
          Man-in-the-Middle (MitM) attack — an attacker secretly intercepts communication between two parties, often on unsecured public Wi-Fi.
          DDoS (Distributed Denial-of-Service) — overwhelming a website or service with traffic so real users can't access it.
          Zero-day exploit — an attack that takes advantage of a software vulnerability before the company that made the software even knows it exists (hence "zero days" to fix it).
          Example: A fake email pretending to be from your bank asking you to "verify your account" is phishing. A program secretly installed on your phone that records everything you type is spyware — a type of malware.

          Why People Get Hacked

          Weak passwords, clicking bad links, reusing credentials, trusting strangers.

          The most common reasons people get hacked:

          1. Reusing passwords across sites — if one site gets breached, attackers try the same password everywhere else.
          2. Clicking links without checking them — urgency and curiosity are a scammer's best tools.
          3. Downloading files from untrusted sources — pirated software and "free" downloads are a top malware delivery method.
          4. Oversharing on social media — birthdays, pet names, and school names are often used as security-question answers.
          5. Ignoring software updates — updates frequently patch security holes that attackers actively exploit.
          6. Falling for urgency-based messages — "act now or lose access" is designed to make you stop thinking critically.

          Studies on real-world breaches consistently find that human error — not sophisticated hacking tools — is behind the large majority of successful attacks.
          Golden rule: Pause before you click. Scammers rely on speed — they want you to act before you think. If a message creates a strong emotional reaction (fear, excitement, urgency), that's itself a warning sign worth slowing down for.

          The CIA Triad

          Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability — the foundation of security.

          The CIA Triad is the foundational model used across the entire cybersecurity field to think about protecting information. It has nothing to do with the intelligence agency — CIA here stands for three core principles:

          Confidentiality — only authorized people can access certain data. Encryption, passwords, and permissions all exist to protect confidentiality.
          Integrity — data hasn't been tampered with or altered without authorization, and can be trusted as accurate.
          Availability — systems and data are accessible to authorized users when they're needed, without unnecessary downtime or disruption.

          Every security decision, tool, or policy maps back to protecting one or more of these three pillars. When evaluating any security measure, it helps to ask: "which of the three is this protecting?"
          Example: Encrypting your messages protects confidentiality (only the intended recipient can read them). A checksum or digital signature on a software download protects integrity (proving the file wasn't altered). A backup of your files protects availability (you can still access your data even after a ransomware attack or hardware failure).

          Scams & Social Engineering

          What is Social Engineering?

          Manipulating people using psychology instead of hacking technology.

          Social engineering attacks exploit human trust, fear, curiosity, and urgency. Attackers impersonate trusted figures such as banks, tech support, or friends to trick you into sharing information, clicking links, or sending money. No software vulnerability is needed — you are the target.
          Example: "Your account will be closed in 24 hours unless you verify your identity now." This uses fear and urgency together.

          Phishing Attacks

          Fake emails and messages that steal your login credentials.

          Phishing messages pretend to be from real companies such as Netflix, Amazon, or your bank. They contain links to fake websites that look identical to real ones. When you enter your password, the attacker captures it. Always check the sender email and URL carefully.
          How to spot it: Hover over links before clicking. Check for misspelled domains such as amaz0n.com or paypaI.com. Real companies never ask for your password by email.

          Vishing & Smishing

          Phone call scams and SMS text message scams.

          Vishing uses phone calls. Attackers pose as bank representatives, government officials, or tech support. Smishing uses text messages with malicious links. Both create panic with messages such as "Your account has been compromised" or "You owe money to the government."
          Defense: Never give personal information over a call you did not initiate. Hang up and call the official number directly.

          Romance & Dating Scams

          Fake relationships built online to steal money.

          Scammers create attractive fake profiles on dating sites and social media. They build emotional connections over weeks or months, then ask for money while citing emergencies such as medical bills, travel costs, or business problems. They often refuse video calls and always have excuses.
          Red flags: They profess love very quickly, can never meet in person, repeatedly experience financial emergencies, and resist video calls.

          Tech Support Scams

          Fake virus warnings that trick you into paying for "fixes."

          Popup messages claim your computer is infected and tell you to call a number. The "technician" asks for remote access and then installs malware, steals data, or charges hundreds of dollars for fake repairs. Real companies such as Microsoft do not show browser popups asking you to call them.
          What to do: Close the browser tab. Never call the displayed number. Run your own trusted antivirus scan.

          Investment & Crypto Scams

          Fake investment platforms that promise guaranteed high returns.

          Scammers create professional-looking websites offering crypto trading, forex, or investment opportunities with guaranteed returns. They display fake dashboards showing growing balances. When you try to withdraw, they ask for additional fees, and your money is gone.
          Rule: If it sounds too good to be true, it probably is. No legitimate investment can guarantee returns. Check whether the platform is registered with a financial regulator.

          What You Should Never Share

          OTPs, passwords, PINs, banking details, and ID numbers.

          Never share OTP codes, passwords, PINs, bank card numbers, CVV codes, ID or passport numbers, recovery codes, or private keys. No legitimate company will ask for these through email, phone, or text.

          Malware Deep Dive

          What is Malware?

          Malicious software designed to damage, spy, or steal.

          Malware is any software intentionally designed to cause harm. It can steal passwords, encrypt files for ransom, spy on activity, display unwanted ads, or give attackers remote control of a device. It spreads through downloads, email attachments, infected websites, and USB drives.

          Viruses & Worms

          Self-replicating programs that spread across devices.

          Viruses attach to legitimate files and activate when opened. They need human action to spread. Worms spread automatically across networks without user interaction. Both can corrupt files, slow systems, and open backdoors for additional attacks.
          Example: The ILOVEYOU worm spread through email and caused billions of dollars in damage worldwide.

          Trojans & RATs

          Malware disguised as legitimate software that opens backdoors.

          Trojans pretend to be useful programs such as free games, cracked software, or fake updates, but secretly install malware. RATs are Remote Access Trojans that give attackers control of a device. They may watch the screen, access the camera, steal files, and record keystrokes.
          Prevention: Download software only from official sources. If an expensive application is offered free by an unknown website, it may contain malware.

          Ransomware

          Encrypts your files and demands payment to unlock them.

          Ransomware encrypts documents, photos, and databases, then demands payment, often in cryptocurrency. Paying does not guarantee file recovery. It commonly spreads through phishing emails, infected downloads, and unpatched vulnerabilities.
          Best defense: Keep regular offline backups. If infected, disconnect the device, avoid paying, report the attack, and restore from a clean backup.

          Spyware & Keyloggers

          Silently monitors everything you do and type.

          Spyware monitors browsing, captures screenshots, and may track location. Keyloggers record keystrokes including passwords, private messages, and payment information. They are often bundled with untrusted software or installed through phishing attacks.
          Possible signs: A device becomes unusually slow, its battery drains quickly, unknown applications appear, or data usage increases without explanation.

          How to Remove Malware

          Step-by-step: disconnect, scan, remove, recover.

          Step 1: Disconnect from the internet. Step 2: Boot into Safe Mode when appropriate. Step 3: Run a trusted full antivirus scan. Step 4: Remove suspicious programs and files. Step 5: Change passwords from a clean device. Step 6: Update all software. Step 7: Monitor accounts for unusual activity.
          // Module 04

          Passwords & Authentication

          Creating Strong Passwords

          Long, random, unique for every account.

          A strong password should be long and unique. Avoid personal information such as names, birthdays, or pet names. Passphrases made from several unrelated words can be strong and easier to remember.
          Test: If someone who knows you could easily guess it, it is not strong enough.

          Password Managers

          Securely store and generate unique passwords for every account.

          Password managers store passwords inside an encrypted vault. You remember one strong master password while the manager creates and saves unique passwords for every account. Many also warn about breached or reused passwords.

          Two-Factor Authentication

          A second verification step that blocks unauthorized access.

          Two-factor authentication adds a second layer after the password, such as a code from an authenticator application, hardware security key, or SMS message. Even if a password is stolen, the attacker still needs the second factor.
          Best practice: Prefer an authenticator application or hardware security key over SMS when available.

          Common Password Attacks

          How attackers crack and steal passwords.

          Brute force: Trying many possible combinations. Dictionary attack: Trying common words and phrases. Credential stuffing: Reusing leaked passwords from other sites. Phishing: Tricking users into entering passwords on fake pages. Keylogging: Recording keystrokes.
          Defense: Use a unique password for every account and enable two-factor authentication.
          // Module 05

          Safe Browsing & Networks

          HTTPS & Encryption

          The padlock icon and what it actually means.

          HTTPS encrypts information between your browser and a website. However, HTTPS alone does not mean the website is legitimate because scam websites can also obtain certificates. Always inspect the domain name as well.

          Public Wi-Fi Dangers

          Why coffee shop Wi-Fi can be a trap.

          On public Wi-Fi, attackers may attempt to intercept unencrypted traffic, capture credentials, inject malicious content, or redirect users to fake websites. Avoid banking or sensitive account activity on untrusted networks.
          Stay safe: Use mobile data for sensitive activity. When public Wi-Fi is necessary, use encrypted websites and a trusted VPN.

          VPNs Explained

          An encrypted tunnel that protects your internet traffic.

          A VPN encrypts internet traffic between your device and the VPN provider's server. It can protect traffic on public Wi-Fi and reduce local network visibility, but it does not make unsafe websites safe or replace antivirus protection.

          Evil Twin Attacks

          Fake Wi-Fi networks that look like the real thing.

          Attackers create Wi-Fi networks with names similar or identical to legitimate networks. When users connect, the attacker may monitor or manipulate traffic.
          Defense: Ask staff for the exact network name, disable automatic connection to open networks, and use a trusted VPN when appropriate.
          // Module 06

          Privacy & Data Protection

          What is Personal Data?

          Name, phone, email, location, photos, passwords, and more.

          Personal data includes information that can identify or describe you, such as your name, phone number, email address, home address, location, photos, birth date, government ID numbers, financial information, biometric data, and browsing history.

          App Permissions

          Why a calculator shouldn't need your microphone.

          Every permission grants an application access to part of your device or data. Review access to the camera, microphone, contacts, location, storage, and phone features. Grant only permissions required for the application's real purpose.

          Data Breaches

          When companies lose your data to hackers.

          A data breach occurs when unauthorized people obtain information stored by an organization. Exposed data may include email addresses, passwords, names, and financial details. Change affected passwords and never reuse the same password across websites.

          Your Digital Footprint

          Everything you do online leaves a trace.

          Posts, searches, likes, purchases, and account activity contribute to your digital footprint. Review privacy settings, avoid publishing sensitive personal details, and periodically inspect what information about you is publicly available.
          // Module 07

          Prevention & Response

          Daily Security Checklist

          Simple habits that prevent most attacks.

          Daily: Avoid suspicious links and lock devices. Weekly: Review permissions and install updates. Monthly: Review account activity and breach alerts. Always: Use unique passwords, enable two-factor authentication, keep backups, and think before sharing information.

          What To Do If Hacked

          Immediate steps to contain the damage.

          1. Change passwords, beginning with your main email account. 2. Enable two-factor authentication. 3. Sign out of unknown devices and sessions. 4. Check for unauthorized transactions. 5. Scan devices for malware. 6. Notify your bank if financial information was exposed. 7. Warn contacts if your accounts were used to send scams. 8. Report the incident.

          Reporting Cyber Crime

          Where and how to report attacks and fraud.

          Report phishing to the organization being impersonated and scams to the relevant local cybercrime authority. Save screenshots, emails, phone numbers, web addresses, and transaction records as evidence.

          Staying Updated & Safe

          Software updates close the doors that hackers use.

          Software updates repair known vulnerabilities. Enable automatic updates for operating systems, browsers, applications, and security tools. Use reputable security software and remain aware of changing scam techniques.
