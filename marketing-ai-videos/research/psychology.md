# Psychology of short marketing video

Research date: 2026-11-05. Each principle gives the **claim**, the **evidence** (with how strong it is), and how to **apply** it in a 15-60s ad, with an example. Strength labels: *strong* (replicated/meta-analysed), *moderate* (solid single studies), *weak* (practitioner convention or vendor data).

---

## 1. Attention and the 1-3 second decision
- **Claim:** viewers judge a piece of content almost instantly and scroll on unless the opening earns another second.
- **Evidence (moderate):** Lindgaard et al. (2006) found people rate the visual appeal of web pages after 50 ms consistently with ratings after longer viewing [1]. Facebook's 2016 feed research (via Fors Marsh Group) found statistically significant recall after 0.25s of mobile feed exposure, and an average of 1.7s spent per piece of mobile content [2]. Caveat: the 0.25s figure is a lab recall threshold, not watch time, and both studies predate the vertical-video era.
- **Apply:** the first frame must already be "the ad" - no logo sting, no slow fade. Put a face, motion, or the product mid-action in frame one, plus a readable on-screen line.
- **Example:** a frame opening on hands peeling a burnt pan clean, with text "This pan was in the bin yesterday."

## 2. Curiosity gaps (information-gap theory)
- **Claim:** curiosity is a feeling of deprivation when people notice a gap between what they know and want to know; they act to close it.
- **Evidence (moderate-strong):** Loewenstein, "The psychology of curiosity: a review and reinterpretation", *Psychological Bulletin* 116(1), 1994 [3]. Curiosity needs some prior knowledge and a gap that is neither too small nor too large; later fMRI work co-authored by Loewenstein is described as finding a "sweet spot" of moderate uncertainty [4].
- **Apply:** pose a specific, answerable question and pay it off inside the ad. Vague teasers ("You won't believe...") read as clickbait; specific gaps ("Why do 3 of 4 standing desks wobble?") recruit prior knowledge.
- **Example:** "Most people set their thermostat wrong at night. Here's the number that cut our bill [X]%." (use your own verified figure). Payoff by second 12.

## 3. Pattern interrupts
- **Claim:** unexpected changes trigger an automatic orienting response that grabs attention.
- **Evidence (moderate):** Lang's Limited Capacity Model (LC4MP) treats structural features like cuts as automatic attention triggers; Lang et al. (1993) found both related and unrelated cuts produced cardiac orienting responses, but unrelated cuts used more processing capacity and memory was better after related cuts [5]. A 2014 eye-tracking study found attention after cuts favours repeated over novel content [5].
- **Apply:** interrupt *visually* (cut, zoom, object entering frame, sound drop) every 2-4s, but keep the interruptions semantically related to the story. Random chaos costs comprehension and memory.
- **Example:** a creator mid-sentence gets splashed by water (interrupt), and the next shot is the waterproof jacket shrugging it off (related payoff).

## 4. Zeigarnik effect and open loops
- **Claim (popular):** people remember unfinished tasks better than finished ones, so open loops keep viewers watching.
- **Evidence (contested):** Ghibellini & Meier's 2025 meta-analysis in *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* concluded the Zeigarnik memory advantage "lacks universal validity", while the **Ovsiankina effect** (the urge to *resume* an interrupted task) is a general tendency [6]. Secondary reports put the recall ratio near 1.0 (no advantage) and resumption around two-thirds (unverified against full text) [6].
- **Apply:** use open loops for *motivation to continue*, not memory. Open a loop early ("Step 3 is the one nobody does"), then close it. Do not rely on unclosed loops to make the brand memorable; unresolved endings frustrate.
- **Example:** list-format ad: "3 mistakes killing your sourdough" with number 3 held until the product appears.

## 5. Peak-end rule
- **Claim:** people judge an experience mostly by its most intense moment and its ending, largely ignoring duration.
- **Evidence (strong, with nuance):** Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber & Redelmeier (1993) found people preferred a longer cold-water trial that ended slightly less painfully [7]. A 2022 meta-analysis (Alaybek et al., *OBHDP*, 174 effect sizes) found strong support for peak-end and a near-null duration effect, but the overall average of the experience predicted evaluations about as well [7]. Applies to experiences with clear starts and ends.
- **Apply:** design one clear emotional peak (the reveal, the transformation, the laugh) and end on a positive, resolved beat with the brand, not on a legal card or a fade. Don't let the last 2s be dead air.
- **Example:** peak = the before/after reveal at second 10; end = satisfied user's line plus logo and offer, cut on a smile.

## 6. Processing fluency vs novelty
- **Claim:** things that are easy to process feel more pleasing and true; but novelty is needed to get noticed. The sweet spot is "most advanced yet acceptable".
- **Evidence (strong for fluency, moderate for MAYA):** Reber, Schwarz & Winkielman (2004, *PSPR*) review evidence that contrast, symmetry, repetition, prototypicality and priming raise fluency and liking [8]. Hekkert, Snelders & van Wieringen (2003, *British Journal of Psychology*) found typicality and novelty jointly predicted aesthetic preference while suppressing each other [9]; at least one student replication was mixed [9].
- **Apply:** be novel in *one* dimension (the hook, the visual idea) and fluent everywhere else: big high-contrast captions, one message, familiar format, consistent brand assets. Novel concept, fluent execution.
- **Example:** a familiar "get ready with me" format (fluent) where the creator is getting a dog ready (novel).

## 7. Story structures that fit 15-60s
Most are practitioner frameworks with little direct experimental evidence (weak); their value is forcing causation and a clear arc.

| Structure | Beats | 30s timing | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **In medias res** | Start at the most dramatic moment, then explain | 0-3s climax, 3-20s how, 20-30s CTA | Natural fit with section 1 |
| **Problem-Agitate-Solve** | Pain, make it vivid, product solves | 0-5 / 5-15 / 15-30 | Copywriting convention; best for direct response |
| **Before/After/Bridge** | Life now, life after, product is the bridge | 0-8 / 8-16 / 16-30 | Show the After visually |
| **StoryBrand (SB7)** | Customer is hero with a problem, brand is guide with a plan and CTA, avoiding failure, reaching success [10] | Hero+problem 0-8, guide 8-15, plan 15-22, CTA 22-30 | Donald Miller, *Building a StoryBrand* (2017); keep the brand as guide, not hero |
| **Pixar story spine** | Once upon a time... Every day... One day... Because of that... Because of that... Until finally... | One line per 5s | Created by improv teacher Kenn Adams, popularised via Emma Coats' 2011 "22 rules" list [11] |
| **But/Therefore** | Link beats with "but" or "therefore", never "and then" [12] | Every beat | Trey Parker & Matt Stone; a test you can run on any script |

- **Apply:** write the script as beats, then check each joint: if "and then" fits, cut or rewrite.
- **Example (but/therefore, 20s):** "I wanted to run every morning, *but* my knees hurt, *therefore* I tried cushioned shoes, *but* they felt like marshmallows, *therefore* we built [X] with a firm heel..."

## 8. Emotion and sharing (high arousal)
- **Claim:** content that evokes high-arousal emotions gets shared more, regardless of valence.
- **Evidence (moderate-strong):** Berger & Milkman, "What makes online content viral?", *Journal of Marketing Research* 49(2), 2012: using New York Times most-emailed data plus experiments, awe, anger and anxiety increased sharing; low-arousal sadness reduced it; positive content was shared more than negative overall [13]. Caveats: the field data is news articles and email sharing, not ads on TikTok.
- **Apply:** aim for awe (spectacle, surprising scale), amusement, or righteous indignation at a shared enemy (a category problem, not a person). Avoid "quietly sad" unless the goal is not sharing.
- **Example:** an anger-at-the-problem hook: "Airlines charge you $40 for this bag. It fits under the seat. Watch."

## 9. STEPPS (Jonah Berger, *Contagious*, 2013)
Six drivers of word of mouth: **Social currency, Triggers, Emotion, Public, Practical value, Stories** [14]. Evidence: synthesis of Berger's research programme (moderate for individual pieces, the framework itself is a book-level synthesis).
- **Apply per element:** Social currency - give viewers an insider fact that makes *them* look smart to share. Triggers - tie the product to a frequent cue (morning coffee, Monday). Emotion - see section 8. Public - make usage visible (distinctive packaging, a gesture). Practical value - a genuinely useful tip in 15s. Stories - wrap the brand inside a narrative people retell (a "Trojan horse").
- **Example:** a 20s tip video "the 2-minute fix for a squeaky door" (practical value) that uses the product naturally, filmed in a recognisable "Saturday chores" moment (trigger).

## 10. Social currency and identity signalling
- **Claim:** people choose and share things that signal who they are, and diverge from majorities in identity-relevant domains.
- **Evidence (moderate):** Berger & Heath (2007, *Journal of Consumer Research*) found people diverge from majority choices in identity domains (music, hairstyles) far more than functional ones (dish soap, power tools); people abandon tastes to avoid signalling an undesired identity [15].
- **Apply:** show the *kind of person* who uses the product, with credible in-group details (jargon, rituals, setting). Don't try to signal every identity at once; that signals none. Beware the flip side: when an out-group adopts a brand, the in-group may leave.
- **Example:** a climbing-shoe ad that uses real crag slang and shows chalk-covered hands on a named route, rather than generic "adventure" stock.

## 11. Why niche beats broad - and the honest tension

### The case for niche creative
- **Specificity:** concrete details are more fluent to the right viewer and act as a filter (sections 2, 6). A hook naming the exact problem self-selects buyers, which is what conversion-optimised delivery (Meta Andromeda, TikTok Smart+) rewards.
- **1,000 True Fans:** Kevin Kelly's 2008 essay argues a creator can make a living from about 1,000 fans each spending about $100/year, an alternative to mass stardom; Kelly says the number is not exact and the model needs direct contact with fans [16]. It is an essay, not empirical evidence (weak), and it applies to creators/small businesses more than to category brands.
- **In-group language** signals identity (section 10).

### The case against pure niche targeting
- **Ehrenberg-Bass / Byron Sharp (*How Brands Grow*):** brands grow mainly through *penetration* (more buyers, including light buyers), and overly narrow targeting works against that [17]. Jenni Romaniuk's work on **distinctive brand assets** (colours, shapes, characters, sounds, judged on *fame* and *uniqueness*) says brands need consistent, recognisable cues that build mental availability across the whole category [17][18].
- **Binet & Field (IPA Databank):** on average about 60:40 brand-building to activation spend works best (later revised to 62:38), and it varies by category [19].

### Reconciling them
These are about different levers. Sharp argues against narrow *media targeting* and inconsistent branding; niche advocates argue for specific *creative*. Platforms in 2026 deliver broadly and let creative find people, so the practical synthesis is:
- **Broad reach, specific creative:** many niche-specific concepts (one per persona or use case), all carrying the *same* distinctive brand assets.
- **Distinctiveness over differentiation in execution:** same colour, sound logo, character or framing every time, so each niche ad also builds the broad brand.
- **Scale matters:** a solo creator or small DTC brand can live on true fans; a mass-market brand cannot. Be honest about which you are.
- **Example:** five 20s ads for a protein bar (nurses on night shift, climbers, parents on the school run, gamers, students in exams), each with niche language, all opening on the same yellow wrapper and ending on the same three-note sound.

## 12. Quick application checklist
- [ ] Frame 1 is already the ad (face/motion/product + text).
- [ ] One specific curiosity gap, paid off inside the ad.
- [ ] Related pattern interrupt every 2-4s.
- [ ] Open loop used to keep watching, then closed.
- [ ] One emotional peak; a resolved, branded ending.
- [ ] Novel in one dimension, fluent in the rest.
- [ ] Script passes the but/therefore test.
- [ ] High-arousal emotion if sharing is a goal.
- [ ] Credible in-group detail for the target identity.
- [ ] Same distinctive brand assets across every niche variant.
